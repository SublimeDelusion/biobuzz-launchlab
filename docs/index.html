UNCERTAINTY IS PART OF THE MODEL

Four shooters · physical gap controls

The lineup is Curved Hood, Two Wheel, Spring-Loaded Straight Ramp, and Underhand. Each has wheel speed and launch angle in The Range. Catapult configurations and observations are archived in exported lab data.

The throat gap is the unloaded clear distance between the working surfaces in the 2D pinch plane. Nominal interference = ball diameter − gap. Positive interference is shared between ball deformation, wheel deformation and, for the straight ramp, carriage motion. Changing wheel diameter keeps the clear gap fixed and moves the axle accordingly. The Two Wheel design divides interference between the two opposed surfaces; each lateral tire shares its group's load.

Underhand follows the mentor’s layout: a vertical bottom feed joins a lower/right guide under an inside powered wheel. Default guide tangents are 30° at entry and 70° at exit, interpreted as angles above horizontal. The 40° upward arc, 65 mm path radius, gap, and wheel placement are provisional. Short tangent sections finish entry and release contact smoothly. Maximum guide arc is limited by the launch angle so the path stays upward and forward at low release angles. The single-wheel inertia and contact leverage are used; there is no assigned spin amount. Normal constraint forces and gravity during contact remain omitted, as in the existing simplified contact model.

The spring carriage is approximated as motion normal to the ramp. A narrower initial gap widens the contact footprint and loads the spring over more of the leading and trailing approach. Its actual contact time also depends on the ball's changing speed. The carriage opens until contact force balances preload plus spring stiffness, or it reaches the travel stop. The model reports maximum opening and travel-stop contact; it does not predict jamming or resolve the full swing linkage.

Shooter exit height defaults to 15 inches and is limited to 18 inches for this team's configuration. This is an application constraint, not a claim about all season expansion rules.

Contact: translation + rotation

Each architecture resolves two opposing tangential contacts. Lateral pairs are projected into one equivalent contact. The Two Wheel layout has a geared front group and powered rear group; total contact load, not wheel count alone, limits traction. The rest gap and wheel geometry determine the contact windows. Wheel-envelope distance limits local compression and contact force; a ball outside the wheel envelope receives no powered traction. The displayed geometry is provisional. The straight ramp has its powered wheels above the ball and reverses the drive torque sign. All mechanical paths include a vertical bottom feed with no powered forces before entry. Ball spin is positive for backspin and negative for topspin. Curved contact geometry is a provisional equivalent path; the solver checks wheel-envelope distance but does not resolve the complete three-dimensional tire deformation field. Each small integration step resolves wheel and stationary-backer slip separately.

Fw = clamp[c (U − v − ω rw-contact), ± μwN]

Local compression sets a spring preload N = kδ × load factor, with no powered load outside the wheel contact envelope. Wheel and backer torque levers depend on lateral spacing. Rail and tape support positions are averaged over their contact widths; total normal load stays independent of area. Contact ends after traveling the arc length.

Both contacts can stick, slip, or reverse friction direction. Reducing contact area alone does not reduce Coulomb friction for a fixed normal load; the tape and rail presets change estimated μ and visible geometry independently.

Flight + opening clearance

Numerical integration includes gravity, quadratic aerodynamic drag, and spin-dependent Magnus lift. Lift saturates as spin ratio increases, and spin decays exponentially.

Fdrag = ½ ρ A Cd |v|²
CL = CMagnus tanh(ωr / |v|)
I = κmr²   (κ = ⅔ for a thin shell)

The 17097 competition preset uses two hub-supported green 2-inch wheels. Both contacts carry the estimated per-wheel traction load. The drive adds a provisional 0.00010 kg·m² of effective shaft inertia to approximate the speed held during the short shot. This is an empirical drivetrain estimate. Loaded wheel RPM from a shot would let the team check it. The 20 mm spacing is interpreted as the clear lateral gap. The 15° hood arc and 75° release angle are independent. Four feet is the reference test distance.

The CELL uses 0.020-inch (0.51 mm) PETG skins fitted into ribs and secured with cable ties. The field uses smooth-side-up EVA foam tiles. The published specifications identify the parts. Their assembled impact properties still need measurement. CELL skins · Assembly guide §6 · Field tiles · POLLEN specifications · Field CAD · Oblique impact research

The entrance is a tilted rectangular plane. The sphere's swept cross-section must clear it across its entire radius, including lateral aim offset. A sphere collides with three finite wall segments, their lip endpoints, and the field floor. A hit near the supported rim and a hit in the middle of a PETG skin can return different amounts of energy. The model gives the rim the shared CELL bounce value (initially 0.65). Between supports it treats part of the sheet as free to recoil. At midspan the assumed effective mass of 0.16 kg gives an effective bounce of about 0.43 for stock POLLEN. The position dependence and effective mass are estimates. A measured surface override replaces this approximation. Friction acts on the sliding speed of the contact patch, including spin. A limited elastic shear response (0.15) permits brief grip and slip reversal, with tangential impulse bounded by friction. Panel vibration after separation, contact with individual hole edges, and movement of the whole HIVE are unresolved.

“Opening entry” is not a guaranteed score. Flexible panels, ball deformation at impact, other balls, and the bistable HIVE tipping are not modeled. Rebounds are illustrative and depend strongly on uncertain wall coefficients. An otherwise identical 1,000 RPM spin test at 6 m/s, 48°, and 1.5 m distance now escapes where the previous wall response settled; this is a model regression case, not a field measurement. The mentor reported frequent backspin-related escapes at competition, including floor bounces and balls leaving the arena. This is a qualitative validation target, not a measured failure-rate calibration.

What to trust, and what to measure

FIRST specifies a 2.8-inch Gopher ResisDent polyethylene ball. AndyMark lists 0.055 lb, approximately 25 g. Integrating the perforated am-5851 solid in FIRST’s field STEP with uniform density gives I/(mr²) of 0.628 to 0.638 along the model axes. The simulation uses the orientation average, 0.635. Spin damping is provisionally 0.03 s⁻¹ (about a 23-second free-air half-life), replacing the earlier 0.15 s⁻¹ estimate. This starting value reflects the report of persistent spin; it is not a measured POLLEN coefficient. It can be changed in Flight physics or replaced using real spin observations. Wall impulses and rolling resistance remain separate from air spin damping.

Perforations may alter drag, lift and spin damping; this model does not resolve airflow through holes and does not assume air adds spin energy. The Magnus coefficient remains an uncalibrated estimate. Measure flight and spin decay separately before treating the predicted effects as calibrated.

Each wheel section has a supplier preset followed by Diameter, Hardness (Shore A), and Compliant depth (mm). Depth is the effective radial rim-to-rigid-core distance, not maximum compression. The AndyMark hex compliant catalog supplies actual 2-inch and 2¼-inch options: green 35A, orange 40A, blue 50A and firm black 60A. The goBILDA 3635-0014-0096 is a 96 mm, 15A FlatTop with a rigid plastic core. Published diameters and hardnesses are distinct from estimated compliant depths: an estimated 8 mm effective rim for AndyMark hex presets, and 20 mm for the gray FlatTop based on the mentor's estimate. None of those depths is a verified manufacturer measurement. Open spokes are represented by equivalent radial compliance, not a resolved structural model. The team confirmed its colored wheels are the 2-inch AndyMark hex version, with no separate plastic hub. In that preset the center hex represents the shaft, not an added plastic hub. The gray FlatTop still has a separate rigid core; the team's custom reinforcement is not separately modeled. Reducing compliant depth is an exploratory way to represent a larger supported core. Existing custom diameter/hardness settings are preserved.

The rear pair is powered. Rear:front shaft ratio 2:1 means front RPM = rear RPM / 2. Wheel diameters independently determine surface speeds. The fixed gearing couples wheel loads and rotational inertia. A single total pinch interference is split equally between opposed surfaces; the rear group support load is resolved through its oblique lateral normals and shared by two tires. Bearing centers lie on a line perpendicular to the release tangent. This replaces the old curved path and offset/overlap windows. Coulomb-limited sliding and a provisional slip conductance determine speed and spin; the solver does not impose the ideal no-slip result. Front and rear contact compliance, the fixed 15 mm lateral gap and 16 mm wheel width are estimates. These results need calibration.

A frictionless stationary guide does not produce a zero-spin launch: the powered wheel applies both forward force and torque. For a thin-shell ball driven at one contact, angular impulse grows with translational impulse. Guide friction opposes local sliding, not spin in isolation. Initially it can add spin; after the ball spins fast enough to slide backward at the guide, friction reverses, helping translation while braking spin. Lower guide friction can therefore leave more spin, depending on contact time, contact leverage, and slip history. Rail presets also change contact geometry, so use the friction slider on one surface to isolate friction alone. The UI reports the computed guide torque's net effect. Rolling and slipping reference. This does not validate the model against the team's observed friction trend; compare measured exit speed and spin before trusting it.

Flywheel inertia is a real model input: two estimated 82 mm, 0.10 kg discs increase the coupled wheel inertia. Contact torque slows the wheels, and added inertia reduces this speed drop. The minimum contact speed and release speed are reported. Motor recovery uses a fixed 80 ms response estimate, rather than a torque-speed curve. Run-up and repeated-shot recovery are not simulated.

Wheel surface speed is determined by RPM and diameter. Exit velocity and spin are estimates sensitive to stiffness, contact load, feed speed, material friction, and motor droop.

Ball stiffness (300,000 N/m), effective wheel width (16 mm), contact conductance, and friction are provisional. Durometer maps to estimated wheel stiffness using k = 17,500 exp((Shore A − 50)/22) × (wheel radius / compliant depth) N/m, in series with ball compliance. At green 35A with default ball stiffness, approximately 96% of local deformation is in the wheel. The same computed split drives tire contact-patch flattening and slight ball deformation. Harder tires transfer more deformation to the ball. The linear wheel model is limited to 65% of compliant depth; bottoming or ball deformation above 1% of diameter flags jam risk and suppresses the shot. These are provisional validity limits, not a measured motor stall prediction; effective depths, stiffness mapping, torque and feed loads are uncalibrated. Carriage travel is accounted for before tire/ball deformation. Shore A is an illustrative input, not a measured constitutive law. The spring carriage is quasi-static with preload, stiffness and a travel stop. The drive estimates 60 g per compliant wheel as a uniform disk and an 80 ms first-order motor speed recovery. Optional 82 mm flywheels add two 0.10 kg uniform disks, reducing contact speed droop. These inertias and recovery times are provisional, not motor specifications.

The ball is physically simulated as a sphere, with local wheel/ball series compliance during launch and spherical collision geometry in flight. A fixed 0.5 ms collision step produces one contact–flight–impact timeline. Playback interpolates those samples; automatic speed and camera movement cannot alter the physics. The normal rebound controls how high the ball bounces off a level tile. Tangential grip trades spin for horizontal speed and can reverse the ball’s direction. High RPM supplies rotational energy, but grip, incoming velocity, and contact location determine whether the ball climbs or escapes. The shot follows the ball after CELL escape, through field-floor bounces, slipping, rolling, and perimeter contact. It ends only at rest, after a complete ball leaves the projected field, or at 20 s. Rolling resistance is provisionally 0.015 and acts only during sustained near-no-slip support; it does not remove spin during impact. For the smooth side of the EVA tile, initial effective friction is 0.45, normal restitution is 0.55, and tangential restitution is 0.12. These are working estimates. At 0.55 normal restitution, a level, spin-free drop returns roughly 30% of its drop height, neglecting drag. Low-speed restitution is suppressed to prevent endless numerical micro-bounces. Gravity and radial constraint forces during wheel contact are omitted. The shot is centered laterally; sidespin, yaw dispersion, and shot-to-shot accuracy statistics are not modeled.

The 2D side projection overlaps the two lateral wheel positions. Slow-motion wheel rotation is sampled from actual RPM. It can still alias visually; the numerical readout is the reliable measure. Results intentionally use modest precision.
